### 11:28 @unknown

Most AI tutorials teach you the model but almost none teach you the retrieval layer underneath it. That retrieval layer is the vector database.  The market passed $2.5 billion in 2025. If you're building anything with LLMs beyond basic chat, you'll run into this infrastructure fast. RAG pipelines, semantic search, and recommendation systems all depend on it.  Traditional databases store structured data in rows and columns and rely on exact matches. Search for "dog training" and you'll only get results containing those exact words. A document about "puppy obedience techniques" won't show up, even though it covers the same thing.  Vector databases store embeddings instead. Models like OpenAI's text-embedding-3 or Sentence Transformers convert your data into numerical vectors that capture meaning. Those two phrases about dog training end up close together in vector space because they carry similar meaning, even though they share zero words.  Your query goes through the same process. The embedding model converts it into a vector, and the database runs approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search to find the closest stored vectors by meaning, not keywords. This keeps retrieval at millisecond speed even across millions of embeddings.  You'll hit vector databases in three places as an AI engineer.  RAG pipelines are the most common entry point. Your LLM doesn't know your company docs, product specs, or last week's meeting notes. A vector database stores all of that as embeddings. When a user asks a question, the system retrieves the most relevant chunks and feeds them to the LLM as context. This grounds answers in real data instead of hallucinations.  Semantic search is the second use case you'll encounter. Traditional keyword search breaks when users use different words than the stored content. Vector search matches by meaning, which is why search in apps, documentation, and support tools is moving to vectors.  Recommendation systems are the third use case. Embedding models turn products, articles, videos, and user profiles into vectors. Similarity between a user's behavior vector and a product vector powers "you might also like" features at scale.  The major purpose-built options include Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, Qdrant, and Milvus. PostgreSQL with pgvector is where most teams start when they want vector search without adding a new database.
